1. WHAT DID PHASE 1 AND PHASE 2 ACTUALLY ACCOMPLISH?
====================================================

Think of it like teaching someone to park a car in two
separate lessons.

PHASE 1 — "Get the car into the car park"
  You only cared about whether the car ended up roughly in
  the right spot (x, y, z position). You didn't care which
  direction the car was facing.

  After 500,000 timesteps the arm learned to navigate the
  workspace and get the probe somewhere near the target.
  It got 5 successes total — meaning 5 times in ~1,700
  episodes the probe actually landed within 2cm of the target.

  That sounds terrible but it's actually meaningful — it means
  the policy learned the general shape of the workspace and how
  joint movements translate to probe movement. The model
  learned "move toward the target" reliably.

PHASE 2 — "Now park AND face the right direction"
  You loaded Phase 1's knowledge and added a new rule:
  once you're within 3cm of the target, also try to rotate
  the probe to the right orientation.

  The big fix here was that before your change, the orientation
  targets were physically impossible for the arm to reach
  (the joint limits problem). After your FK fix, the targets
  are always reachable.

  Phase 2 got 0 successes but the distance improved — best
  episodes got down to 7cm. The orientation reward never even
  activated (it only turns on inside 3cm) because the arm
  couldn't consistently get that close yet.

WHAT DO THE RESULTS TELL YOU?
  Phase 1 result:
    The arm can learn to navigate. 5 successes in 500k steps
    with position-only training is a proof of concept.

  Phase 2 result:
    The arm is getting closer (7cm vs starting at ~30cm) but
    hasn't cracked the final 2cm consistently. The FK fix is
    working — orientation targets are now valid — but the
    position reward just needs more training time or a stronger
    signal to push below 2cm.

2. FORWARD KINEMATICS VS INVERSE KINEMATICS
====================================================

THE BEST ANALOGY: YOUR ARM REACHING FOR A CUP

FORWARD KINEMATICS (FK) — "where does my hand end up?"
  You know the angles of your shoulder, elbow, and wrist.
  FK answers: given those angles, where is your hand in space?

    Shoulder at 45°, Elbow at 90°, Wrist at 30°
                  ↓
             FK calculation
                  ↓
        Hand is at position (x=0.4m, y=0.3m, z=1.1m)
        facing direction (quaternion)

  Easy direction. One set of joint angles → one unique answer.
  No ambiguity.

  In code this is getLinkState() — PyBullet does the FK math
  for you automatically when you set joint angles.

INVERSE KINEMATICS (IK) — "what angles do I need to reach the cup?"
  You see a cup at position (0.4, 0.3, 1.1).
  What shoulder/elbow/wrist angles get your hand there?

    Target position: (x=0.4m, y=0.3m, z=1.1m)
                  ↓
             IK calculation
                  ↓
        Solution A: Shoulder 45°, Elbow 90°, Wrist 30°
        Solution B: Shoulder 60°, Elbow 70°, Wrist 15°
        Solution C: ... (many valid answers)
        OR: "impossible, can't reach" (joint limits exceeded)

  Hard direction. One target position → potentially many joint
  angle solutions, or none at all. That's why IK is tricky —
  and why Unity ragdolls and Boston Dynamics use special solvers
  to pick the "best" solution.

REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES:
  - GPS navigation uses FK thinking:
    "given I'm at this road junction and I drove 500m north,
     where am I now?"

  - Reaching for a shelf uses IK thinking:
    "the shelf is there — how do I position my arm to grab it?"

  - Animation in games (Unity/Unreal ragdolls):
    IK is used so a character's hand automatically reaches toward
    a door handle regardless of where the character is standing.
    The animator says "hand goes HERE" — IK figures out the
    elbow and shoulder angles.

  - Boston Dynamics robot dog:
    IK is used for foot placement. The controller says
    "put your foot on that rock" and IK figures out which
    leg joint angles achieve that.

IN THIS PROJECT:
  - Your FK-based target sampling uses FK:
    Pick random joint angles → run FK → get the probe position
    → use that as the target. Guaranteed reachable.

  - PyBullet has a built-in IK solver:
    calculateInverseKinematics(robot, end_link, target_pos, target_orn)
    Not currently used in the RL training, but available.

  - Your IK idea (from project_deep_dive.txt Section 22):
    Using IK to warm-start the agent near the target, then
    letting RL handle fine adjustment and collision avoidance.
    This is a valid research direction called "IK-initialised RL."

4. HOW MANY TOTAL EPISODES RAN?
====================================================

Each episode ends at max 300 steps (or earlier on success).

PHASE 1:
  500,000 steps ÷ ~290 steps/episode avg = ~1,700 episodes
  Successes: 5

PHASE 2 (across all sessions):
  Session 5: 350,000 steps  → ~1,170 episodes
  Session 6: 150,000 steps  → ~500 episodes
  Total Phase 2: ~1,670 episodes
  Successes: 0

COMBINED TOTAL: ~3,400 episodes across both phases.

WHY SO FEW SUCCESSES?
  The task is genuinely hard. Each episode is a random new
  target anywhere in the clinical workspace. The arm starts
  from zero every time. Getting within 2cm of a random 3D
  target, with 9 independent joints and complex kinematics,
  in 300 steps — that's the challenge.

  The success rate will improve significantly as:
  a) More training steps accumulate
  b) The reward signal is tuned to push the arm closer
  c) (Eventually) orientation training activates inside the gate

5. ABSOLUTE POSITION vs RELATIVE DISTANCE — WHY BOTH?
====================================================

Think of it like GPS vs a compass.

  RELATIVE DISTANCE (index 0 in the observation):
    Like a compass that says "target is 0.22m away."
    Tells the agent HOW FAR — but not WHICH WAY.

  ABSOLUTE POSITIONS (indices 5-10):
    Like GPS coordinates. Tells the agent WHERE things
    are in 3D space.

YOU NEED BOTH:

  Scenario: probe is 0.22m from target

  With ONLY distance (compass):
    Agent knows:        "I'm 0.22m away"
    Agent doesn't know: "am I above it? left? behind it?"
    Result:             Can't decide which direction to move

  With BOTH distance + absolute positions (compass + GPS):
    Agent knows: "I'm at (0.1, 0.5, 0.2),
                  target is at (-0.05, 0.45, 0.3)"
    Agent can figure out: move left, forward, and up

COULD THE NETWORK COMPUTE DISTANCE ITSELF FROM THE POSITIONS?
  Technically yes — distance = sqrt((x2-x1)^2 + (y2-y1)^2 + (z2-z1)^2)

  But giving it the pre-computed distance makes learning faster.
  The network doesn't have to spend training steps figuring out
  the Pythagorean theorem. You're giving it the answer to the
  easy part so it can focus on the hard part
  (which joint angles produce the right movement).

  This is a common design principle in RL: give the agent
  as much relevant information as possible in the observation.
  More information = less to figure out from scratch.

6. SECTION 9 — ACTION SPACE (CLEARER, WITH VISUALS)
====================================================

The neural network outputs 10 numbers, one per joint.
Think of it like a mixing board with 10 sliders:

  NEURAL NETWORK OUTPUT (raw, always between -1 and +1):
  ┌─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┬─────┐
  │ J0  │ J1  │ J2  │ J3  │ J4  │ J5  │ J6  │ J7  │ J8  │ J9  │
  │-0.2 │+0.7 │+0.7 │-0.1 │+0.5 │-0.8 │+0.3 │-0.4 │+0.6 │-0.1 │
  └─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┴─────┘

  AFTER SCALING (what actually gets sent to PyBullet):
  ┌─────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ J0  │ -0.2 ÷ 4  = -0.05 rad  (gantry rail, small scale)   │
  │ J1  │ +0.7 × π  = +2.20 rad  (rotational joint)           │
  │ J2  │ OVERRIDDEN = -2.20 rad  (always = -J1, coupled gear) │
  │ J3  │ -0.1 × π  = -0.31 rad                               │
  │ J4  │ +0.5 × π  = +1.57 rad                               │
  │ J5  │ -0.8 × π  = -2.51 rad                               │
  │ J6  │ +0.3 × π  = +0.94 rad                               │
  │ J7  │ -0.4 × π  = -1.26 rad                               │
  │ J8  │ +0.6 × π  = +1.88 rad                               │
  │ J9  │ -0.1 × π  = -0.31 rad                               │
  └─────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

WHY DIVIDE J0 BY 4 INSTEAD OF MULTIPLY BY PI?
  J0 is a LINEAR rail (slides up and down like a drawer).
  Multiplying by π would move it ~3.14 metres — enormous.
  The whole arm would slide completely off the rail.
  Dividing by 4 keeps it in a sensible range (±0.25m).

WHY IS J2 OVERRIDDEN?
  This models a real parallelogram linkage in the physical robot.
  Two arm segments are connected by a gear/linkage mechanism that
  forces them to always move in opposite directions — like a
  scissor-lift. You can't control them independently.
  The RL agent outputs a value for J2 but it gets ignored.

  In code (run_phase2.py):
    scaled[0] = action[0] / 4         ← gantry
    scaled[i] = action[i] * np.pi     ← all rotational joints
    scaled[2] = -scaled[1]            ← coupling override

WHAT HAPPENS AFTER SCALING?
  PyBullet receives these scaled angles as TARGET POSITIONS
  for each joint's motor controller. The motor tries to reach
  that angle during the 3 simulation steps. It's position
  control — like telling a servo "go to 45 degrees" rather
  than "apply X force."

HOW THE EPISODE LOOKS STEP BY STEP:
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Episode start: all joints at 0°, random target   │
  │                                                  │
  │  Step 1:  Network sees obs → outputs 10 numbers  │
  │           → scale → send to PyBullet             │
  │           → sim runs 3 ticks → read new position │
  │           → compute reward → store in buffer      │
  │                                                  │
  │  Step 2:  Repeat with new obs                    │
  │  ...                                             │
  │  Step 300: Timeout (truncated) or success        │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

7. SECTION 10 — REWARD FUNCTION (CLEARER, WITH VISUALS)
====================================================

Think of reward like a score in a video game. The agent wants
to maximise its total score over the entire episode.

--- PHASE 1 REWARD ---

EVERY SINGLE TIMESTEP, the agent gets scored like this:

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ EXAMPLE: arm is 0.22m from target this step,        │
  │          was 0.24m last step                        │
  │                                                     │
  │ TERM 1 — Distance penalty (always active):          │
  │   dist_normalised = 0.22 / 0.7533 = 0.29           │
  │   reward += -0.29                                   │
  │   (0.7533m is the diagonal of the workspace —       │
  │    the maximum possible distance, so this           │
  │    normalises to 0-1 range)                         │
  │                                                     │
  │ TERM 2 — Progress bonus (did I get closer?):        │
  │   progress = 0.24 - 0.22 = +0.02m                  │
  │   reward += 5.0 × 0.02 = +0.10                     │
  │                                                     │
  │ TERM 3 — Success jackpot (am I at target?):         │
  │   0.22m > 0.02m threshold → NO                     │
  │   reward += 0                                       │
  │                                                     │
  │ TOTAL THIS STEP: -0.29 + 0.10 + 0 = -0.19          │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

OVER A FULL EPISODE (300 steps):
  If the arm wanders:   many -0.3 steps → total ≈ -90
  If the arm improves:  progress bonuses offset penalties → ≈ -50
  If it reaches target: +100 jackpot → total could be +20 or higher

WHY THE 5.0 MULTIPLIER ON PROGRESS?
  Without it:
    Being 0.3m away    → penalty of -0.40
    Moving 0.01m closer → bonus of +0.01
    The penalty dominates. The agent barely cares about moving.

  With 5.0 multiplier:
    Being 0.3m away    → penalty of -0.40
    Moving 0.01m closer → bonus of +0.05
    Progress becomes meaningful. Active movement is rewarded.

  The ×5 multiplier makes the agent "want" to chase the target
  rather than just passively exist near it.

--- PHASE 2 REWARD — THE GATE ---

  VISUALISING THE GATE:

  Far from target           Near target (< 3cm)    At target
  ───────────────────────────────────[GATE]──────────────●
                                        ↑
                                     3cm = 0.03m

  OUTSIDE GATE (dist > 3cm):
    Only position reward is active:
      reward = -dist_n + 5.0 × progress

  INSIDE GATE (dist < 3cm):
    Position reward stays ON, PLUS orientation kicks in:
      reward = -dist_n + 5.0 × progress       ← same as before
             + (-0.05 × angle_normalised)     ← orientation penalty
             + (0.5  × angle_progress)        ← orientation progress bonus

  THE DRIFT PENALTY:
    If you were inside the gate last step but drifted outside this step:
      reward -= 10.0

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ WHY THE GATE EXISTS:                                │
  │                                                     │
  │ Imagine trying to aim a laser pointer while also    │
  │ running toward a moving target. You can't do both   │
  │ well at the same time. The gate says:               │
  │                                                     │
  │ "Get close first. THEN worry about the angle."      │
  │                                                     │
  │ Without the gate, the agent gets a confusing mix of │
  │ position and orientation signals from step 1, and   │
  │ learns neither properly.                            │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ WHY THE DRIFT PENALTY EXISTS:                       │
  │                                                     │
  │ The agent got inside the gate (dist < 3cm).         │
  │ It starts adjusting orientation.                    │
  │ In doing so, it wiggles a joint and drifts to 4cm.  │
  │                                                     │
  │ Without drift penalty:                              │
  │   Agent doesn't notice — it still got some          │
  │   orientation reward. Drifting seems acceptable.    │
  │                                                     │
  │ With drift penalty (-10):                           │
  │   Drifting costs MORE than any orientation reward   │
  │   gained. Agent learns: stay put while you rotate.  │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

--- SUCCESS CONDITION (Phase 2) ---

  Euclidean distance < 0.02m   (2cm)
  AND
  Quaternion angle error < 1.2 rad  (~68°)
    → reward += 100.0
    → episode terminates

  The 1.2 rad tolerance is intentionally relaxed (original was 0.2 rad)
  so the agent can actually see success during early training.
  It will be tightened gradually in later training stages.

--- VISUALISING THE FULL REWARD LANDSCAPE ---

  HIGH REWARD territory (what the agent is trying to reach):
  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │                                                   │
  │  ● TARGET (0cm, perfect orientation)   reward ≈ +100 (jackpot)
  │  ○ Inside gate, good orientation       reward ≈ -5 to +20 per ep
  │  ○ Inside gate, bad orientation        reward ≈ -30 per ep
  │  ○ Outside gate, moving toward it      reward ≈ -50 per ep
  │  ○ Far away, not improving             reward ≈ -90 per ep
  │  ✗ Drifted out while in gate           reward ≈ -100 per ep
  │                                                   │
  └───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  The agent starts at the bottom and tries to climb to the top.
  Every training episode it gets a little better at climbing.
